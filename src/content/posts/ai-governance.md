AI governance has an odd problem. Almost everyone agrees that it is necessary, but the moment an organisation tries to put it into practice, it has a tendency to become paperwork.

There are understandable reasons for this. Regulators want accountability. Boards want assurance. Compliance teams need evidence. Risk functions need classifications, controls and documented ownership. The natural response is to create policies, committees, approval processes and, increasingly, an inventory of the AI systems operating across the organisation.

None of that is wrong.

The problem is when governance starts there and ends there.

## The register, two ways

An AI register is a good example. It can be treated in two very different ways. In one version, it is an annual exercise: departments are asked which AI systems they use, somebody consolidates the responses, missing information is chased down, and the result becomes the organisation's official record.

In the other, the register begins much earlier — when an AI system first enters the organisation, or before it moves from experimentation into actual use.

The difference sounds procedural. It is not.

If the register is assembled retrospectively, governance is describing the organisation after the fact. If registration happens before deployment, governance becomes part of how the organisation decides what is allowed to happen in the first place.

That distinction matters more as AI becomes easier to build and easier to distribute.

## From two systems to twenty

When an organisation has two AI systems in production, formal governance can almost feel unnecessary. The people involved probably know what the systems do, where the data comes from, who owns them and what happens when something goes wrong.

At twenty systems, that informal knowledge begins to disappear.

Some applications were built internally. Others came from vendors. AI capabilities appeared inside software the organisation already used. Employees created copilots. Business teams experimented with external tools. A proof of concept quietly became useful enough to enter a real workflow.

None of this necessarily implies poor judgement. It reflects something more ordinary: technology can now spread through an organisation faster than the organisation can build a complete picture of it.

## Deployment questions in disguise

That is where the register becomes more interesting than it first appears.

Look at the questions a useful AI register contains:

- Who owns the system?
- What data does it process?
- What can it access?
- Is it customer-facing?
- Does it make or influence decisions?
- How much autonomy does it have?
- What happens when it produces an incorrect output?
- Who is expected to intervene?
- What evidence exists to reconstruct what happened later?

These look like compliance questions because compliance teams are often the ones collecting the answers.

But they are really deployment questions.

If an organisation considers them only during an annual inventory exercise, it is discovering information about its AI estate that ideally should have been established before the system was put into use.

## Register first

That suggests a different way to think about the register.

Rather than treating it as the final record of the governance process, make it one of the first steps.

Before an AI system moves into meaningful use, it is identified. An owner is named. Its intended purpose is recorded. Its data and system access are understood. Its level of autonomy is assessed. The relevant risks are classified, and the organisation determines which controls, approvals and oversight are appropriate.

The register then stops being a spreadsheet that tries to reconstruct history.

It becomes part of the organisation's memory of why a system was allowed to exist in the form that it does.

## Systems don't stay still

That matters because AI systems do not remain static after approval.

Models change. Prompts change. New data sources are connected. Vendors release new capabilities. A pilot becomes part of a workflow. A system that originally supported a human decision may gradually begin to influence more of the process.

A governance framework that records a system once and then waits for the next annual review is therefore always at risk of looking backwards.

The more useful question is not simply whether the organisation has documented the system, but whether the documentation continues to describe the system that is actually operating.

## Human oversight

This is also where human oversight becomes more complicated than it appears on paper.

"Human in the loop" is one of the most common responses to AI risk, and for good reason. Important decisions should not become effectively autonomous simply because a model can produce them faster.

But human oversight is not created merely by adding an approval step.

If one employee receives hundreds of AI-generated decisions every day, the organisation may technically have a human in the loop. Whether that person is exercising meaningful judgement is a different question.

The design problem is therefore not just where the human sits in the process.

It is what that person is expected to understand, which decisions actually require review, what information they need in order to challenge the system, how often intervention should happen, and how much one person can realistically supervise.

That is the difference between documenting a control and making the control operational.

## The operating layer

And it points to what I think is the harder part of AI governance.

The difficult work often sits somewhere between policy and engineering.

Policies can define what should happen. Engineering teams can build systems that behave in particular ways. But organisations still need an operating layer that connects the two:

- When does a system need to be registered?
- Who decides its risk classification?
- What evidence is required before it can move forward?
- What constitutes a material change?
- When does a system need to be reviewed again?
- Who can accept the remaining risk?
- Who can stop deployment?

These are not particularly glamorous questions. But they determine whether governance works in practice.

Without that operating layer, governance tends to become periodic.

The technology does not.

## Closer to the point of change

That is why I think the most useful shift is not to add more governance, but to move governance closer to the point where change happens.

Register the system before it becomes operational. Update the record when its capabilities, data access or role materially change. Keep ownership current. Make the relevant approvals part of the deployment process rather than a later reconciliation exercise.

The annual inventory can still exist. Regulators may require it. Boards may want it.

But by then it should be the easy part.

It should be the output of a governance process that has been running throughout the year, not the moment when the organisation begins trying to remember what happened.

That, to me, is the practical challenge with AI governance.

The policies matter. The principles matter. The committees matter.

But the real test is simpler:

> **Does governance happen before an AI system does something consequential, or after?**
